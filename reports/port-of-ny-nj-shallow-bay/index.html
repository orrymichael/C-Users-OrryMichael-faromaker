The port backdrop

The Port of NY/NJ ranked as the third-busiest U.S. port at mid-year 2026, handling 4.4M TEUs in H1 — good for an 18.1% share of volume among the top nine reporting ports. Loaded import volume reached 2.2M TEUs. The port moved $264B in goods in 2024 and provides access to more than 60 million consumers within a 250-mile radius, roughly one-third of the nation's GDP. Rail cargo totaled 341,671 containers in H1 2026 — meaningful, but still a minority share against truck-dependent flows. As trucking rates continue to rise, occupiers are increasingly prioritizing locations closer to the port to reduce drayage costs — a trend the Port Authority is reinforcing through infrastructure investment, including a landmark agreement to evaluate deepening navigational channels to 55 feet and a major redevelopment of the Newark-Elizabeth port complex's northern entrance.

Why this matters for NNJ specifically

Northern New Jersey sits directly in that truck-served radius — the closest, most built-out industrial geography to the port complex on the New Jersey side. The Port Region submarket itself is the largest in Northern NJ, with 84.1 million square feet of warehouse inventory. That proximity is precisely why NNJ carries some of the highest real estate and labor costs in the country: it's the price of access to the East Coast's leading container gateway.

More than distribution: what shallow bay actually houses

Import volume doesn't just create demand for warehousing — it seeds an entire ecosystem of businesses that need small-bay space to operate. Shallow-bay buildings in NNJ house last-mile distribution, light manufacturing and assembly, contractor and trade businesses (electrical, plumbing, HVAC, millwork), showroom and wholesale operations, food and beverage prep and distribution, e-commerce fulfillment and returns processing, and import-adjacent light assembly (kitting, packaging, light customization of goods landing at the port). These tenants are typically smaller local and regional operators, not national logistics users — part of why shallow-bay leasing is granular, relationship-driven, and less correlated to any single tenant's fortunes than large-format bulk space.

Where Faropoint fits

This is the exact dynamic Faropoint's NNJ platform is built around: high-frequency acquisition of infill, supply-constrained shallow-bay assets serving this diversified tenant base — not large-format bulk product built for a handful of logistics users. Faropoint is the No. 1 buyer in NJ's industrial market by deal count YTD 2026 — 5 deals, 9.8% of all tracked NJ industrial, land and IOS deals, widening to 19.2% when narrowed to industrial-sales transactions specifically. That deal flow gives the platform outsized, and growing, visibility into the off-market, relationship-driven activity this thesis depends on.

The takeaway for LPs. The story isn't "industrial is a good asset class," and it isn't just "distribution near ports is good." It's that port throughput seeds demand across a wide range of light-industrial, trade, and distribution users, all competing for the same scarce shallow-bay stock — in a market where rents keep climbing even as some new supply comes online. Faropoint's leading share of NJ deal volume shows the platform capturing that demand deal by deal, and its diversified tenant exposure means its fortunes aren't tied to any single tenant type or industry cycle.

Why shallow bay|The structural case — and where it shows up regionally

Port proximity is one input, not the whole case. Six structural drivers explain why shallow bay specifically captures a disproportionate share of port-driven demand, regardless of any single trade cycle.

1Last-mile, not just port-adjacent

Final-mile parcel and distribution demand is driven by dense population and same-day delivery expectations — a driver independent of ocean-freight cycles.

2Land that can't be replaced

Under-construction inventory is ~4x higher in big box (2.6% of stock) than last-mile (0.6%) — zoning and land cost structurally cap new shallow-bay supply (Faropoint Research).

3Granular, diversified tenancy

Dozens of small, service-oriented tenants per roll — trades, light manufacturing, distribution — spread credit and re-tenanting risk versus one large user.

4Rents — and yields — mark to market faster

Shorter lease terms let owners reprice more often; the same dynamic shows in cap rates — urban logistics (20–100K SF) trades ~70 bps wide of big box (Faropoint Research).

5Fragmented ownership, our entry point

79% of the 20–100K SF universe ($1.1T sector, avg. asset value $5M) is privately owned — a durable, largely un-institutionalized acquisition pipeline (Faropoint Research).

6Demand from more than one source

Population growth, reshoring of light manufacturing and local service businesses all draw on the same infill space — insulating it from any single macro shock.

Distribution channels|Why every channel converges on shallow bay

None of the five channels on the previous page are actually competing on volume — they're competing on proximity and divisibility, and 20–100K SF is the format that satisfies both.

E-commerce fulfillment

Large-scale primary fulfillment does sit in big-box, but returns processing, regional sortation, and the long tail of smaller/niche sellers don't generate enough volume to fill 200K+ SF — and same-day/next-day promises push them toward space near the population they're shipping to, not toward the cheapest big parcel two hours away.

Wholesale & 3PL

Regional 3PLs serve a defined local client base and territory. Their footprint scales with that territory's demand, not with national throughput — so they need space sized to a region, not a continent, and they need it divisible so it can flex up or down as clients turn over.

Last-mile logistics

This one is definitional: last-mile economics are won by minimizing drive time to the end customer, which means many small nodes near dense population beats one large node far away. A single 500K SF facility in Meadowlands wouldn't out-perform five 60K SF depots spread across it — density of locations matters more than size of any one location.

Light manufacturing & assembly

Kitting, packaging, light assembly of import components — these are small production operations, not heavy industry. They need modest space for a production line plus staging, want to be near their labor pool and near the port/rail input they're processing, and simply don't have the throughput to justify bulk-scale space.

Trade services & contractors

HVAC, plumbing, electrical, millwork. These are local service businesses by nature — they need a yard/warehouse to stage materials and park trucks in each market they serve, not one central depot. Real estate need is capped by "how much can one crew's territory support," which is small.

The structural reason

It isn't just that these tenants want to be small — the supply of anything bigger is structurally rationed in these infill submarkets. Under-construction inventory runs ~4x higher for big-box (2.6% of stock) than for shallow bay (0.6%) because land cost and zoning cap new small-format supply near dense population.

Scarce land, shared appetite. Five very different tenant types — none of them competing with each other directly — end up bidding for the same limited shallow-bay stock. That's the common denominator from the previous page, and the reason the diagram converges all five into one hero node instead of five separate outcomes.
